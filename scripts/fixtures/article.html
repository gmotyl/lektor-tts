The Reading Room

A short history of reading aloud

By A. Writer · 6 min read

For most of history, reading was something you did with your voice. Ancient readers spoke the words on the page, even when they were alone, and a silent reader was rare enough to be remarked on.

Texts were written to be heard. Scrolls had no spaces between words and little punctuation, so the reader's voice did the work of finding where one thought ended and the next began.

Reading aloud was also social. A household gathered in the evening to hear a letter, a newspaper or the next chapter of a novel, and one good reader could bring a story to a whole room.

The quiet turn

Silent reading spread with cheaper books and private rooms. It is faster, and it lets the reader skim, pause and return at will, so for many tasks it simply won.

Yet listening never went away. Radio dramas, audiobooks and podcasts all show how much we still enjoy a voice carrying us through a text while our hands and eyes are busy elsewhere.

Machines that read

Early speech synthesizers sounded flat and mechanical, but they opened the written web to people who could not see the screen. Modern engines are far more natural, and they can run on a home computer.

Following the highlighted sentence while it is read keeps your place on the page. It turns any article into something you can listen to, look up from, and pick up again without losing the thread.
